# Content quality

The part that decides whether you rank, and the part agents are worst at.

## The standard

Google's helpful-content assessment moved into core ranking in March 2024 and now runs
continuously as a **site-wide** signal. Two consequences:

1. Thin pages don't merely fail to rank — they lower the assessment of the whole domain.
2. Recovery is gradual and requires lifting quality across the site, not deleting one bad
   page.

The test Google frames it with: would this content exist, in this form, if search engines
didn't? And would someone who came to the site directly find it useful?

## E-E-A-T

Experience, Expertise, Authoritativeness, Trustworthiness. Not a score — a description of
what quality raters look for. Google states **trust is the most important**, and the others
exist to support it.

For a small business site, that translates concretely:

- **Experience** — evidence the work was actually done. Project photos, specific problems
  encountered, what it cost, what went wrong. This is the letter small businesses can win
  on and big competitors can't fake.
- **Expertise** — the writing knows the trade. Correct terminology, real sequence of
  operations, honest about what doesn't work.
- **Authoritativeness** — who is behind this. Named people, real bios, real history.
- **Trustworthiness** — real contact details, real address, honest pricing language, no
  fabricated credentials, clear policies.

## Rules for agents

These are written for our specific failure modes.

### Never fabricate

The single hardest rule and the most important one. You will be asked to write a service
page and you will not have been given the warranty length, the years in business, the
brands used, or the price range. **Do not invent them.**

- Write about how the work is properly done — scope, sequence, what fails when steps are
  skipped. That is true regardless of the business, genuinely useful, and demonstrates
  expertise.
- Leave business-specific claims to a data file that the owner fills in, and render them
  only when populated.
- **Keep a list of every fact you couldn't verify and hand it to the human.** Don't quietly
  write around a gap.

A fabricated trust claim is worse than a missing one. It's the exact opposite of the letter
Google says matters most, and for regulated trades it's a legal problem too.

### Write from specifics

A page that could describe any business in any town describes nothing. Before shipping any
page, find the sentence that could only appear on *this* page. If there isn't one, the page
isn't ready.

Banned as load-bearing content: "beautiful homes", "discerning homeowners", "unique
architectural character", "state-of-the-art", "unparalleled quality", "we pride ourselves".
These are the tell that a template wrote the page.

### Answer first

Lead with the answer, then support it. The first two sentences should resolve the question
in the title.

This serves three things at once: readers who are scanning, featured snippets, and AI
assistants deciding what to quote. A page ranking #4 can be the one an assistant cites
because it answered most cleanly.

### Structure for extraction

- Descriptive `h2`/`h3` that state what the section answers
- Short paragraphs; lists where the content is genuinely a list
- Tables for comparisons — they get quoted
- Define terms on first use
- Put the specific number, range, or condition in the text, not implied

### Length

There is no target word count. There is a target: **completely answer the question and
stop.** In practice a real service page lands around 800–1,500 words because that's what
the subject needs. Padding to hit a number is exactly what the thin-content assessment
detects.

Under ~250 words of body text, a page is usually not a page. Make it a section of another
page instead.

### Disclose automation where it's material

Google's guidance asks whether the use of AI generation is self-evident, whether background
is provided, and why automation was useful. Using AI to *produce many pages for the primary
purpose of manipulating rankings* is a spam violation. Using it to help write genuinely
useful content is not. Know which one you're doing.

## Topic selection

For a small site, pick topics by intent, highest return first:

1. **Transactional** — "cabinet refinishing sarasota". Few queries, highest value. Owned by
   service pages and a small number of hand-written location pages.
2. **Commercial investigation** — "cost to paint a house in florida", "refinish vs replace
   cabinets". Larger volume, real intent, and the biggest underexploited opportunity for
   most local businesses. Owned by guides.
3. **Informational** — "why does paint peel off stucco". Builds topical authority and
   attracts links and citations. Owned by guides.

Cluster them: a service page as the hub, guides as spokes, bidirectional links, spokes
cross-linking to genuine siblings. Nothing important more than three clicks from home.

## Reviewing content someone else wrote

- Find the unverifiable claims. List them.
- Find the sentences that could appear on a competitor's site verbatim. Those are filler.
- Check whether the first paragraph answers the title's question.
- Check whether any two pages are answering the same query — if so, they're competing with
  each other and one should be merged or re-pointed.
- Check that headings describe content rather than decorating it.
